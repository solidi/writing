# Review Context — Spoke

Voice guide for deep product reviews and practical buying guides in a niche hobby domain. Covers optics, gear, tools, instruments, and collectible equipment where an enthusiast reader is choosing what to buy and the essay is the authority that helps them choose. Read [ai/writing-context.md](writing-context.md) first. Baseline exemplar: [111-how-to-become-a-knowledgeable-binoculars-enthusiast-without-spending-a-lot-of-money.my.md](../111-how-to-become-a-knowledgeable-binoculars-enthusiast-without-spending-a-lot-of-money.my.md).

## When to load this file

- A buying guide for a category of physical gear: binoculars, telescopes, cameras, turntables, knives, pens, watches, headphones, tools, instruments.
- A survey review that names specific makes and models, prices them, and ranks them by use case.
- A hobby-entry guide: "how to become knowledgeable in X without spending a lot of money."
- A spec-literacy piece that teaches the reader how to read a product's numbers.
- A vintage / secondary-market guide for a collectible product category.
- A contrarian take against received wisdom inside a hobby community.

Do **not** load this file for a software tool review. A take on an IDE, a language, a framework, or a developer product routes to [software-context.md](software-context.md), structure E. The review spoke is for **physical goods bought with money by a hobbyist**, where the reader's decision has a price tag and a return window.

If the product category is a toy or a toy-adjacent collectible, the toy spoke wins — that work is archival history, not purchase advice. If a review essay reconstructs the manufacturing history of the category, borrow the history spoke's mechanism-explanation discipline on top of this file.

## Voice

- **Hobbyist authority.** The narrator is not a reflective practitioner (software) or an archivist-custodian (toy) or a regional storyteller (history). The narrator is the person at the club meeting who owns fourteen of the things and will tell you which two to buy. Confidence is the register.
- **First-person singular, unhedged.** "I insist on selecting BaK4." "I recommend porro-prism binoculars over roof-prism binoculars." "I would call these binoculars microscopes." No "this author" — that is the toy and history distancing device and it is wrong here. The whole value of the piece is that a named person is putting their taste on the line.
- **Direct address to the reader.** Second person carries the recommendations: "get a pair of Nikon Trailblazers", "If you can find one in excellent condition on the secondary market, grab it", "keep comfort in mind." The reader is a peer who is about to spend money.
- **Contrarian toward the category's experts.** Every review-spoke essay should stake at least one position against the consensus of its hobby. "In this area of expertise, I find myself disagreeing with the so-called experts." "Regardless of what experts say, low-magnification zoom bins have their place." "Very expensive binoculars are not what they are cracked up to be." The heterodoxy is the reason the piece exists.
- **Generous, not gatekeeping.** The essay lowers the cost of entry. It names the cheap thing that works and the expensive thing that does not. Snobbery is the enemy of this voice.
- **Concessive.** "Don't get me wrong." "This is where I depart from the strict rule that …" The narrator names the exception to their own rule rather than pretending the rule is universal. Self-contradiction, owned aloud, reads as expertise.

## Document anatomy (canonical layout)

Baseline is essay 111. Treat as the default skeleton; deviate with cause.

1. **Category banner** as a top-of-file H2: `## ON BINOCULARS`. Names the product category, not the hobby.
2. **Title** as H1. See "Titles" below.
3. **Subtitle** as H2 directly under the title. Short and functional: "A Practical Guide". The review spoke's subtitle names the essay's *form*, not its thesis — unlike the history spoke, where the subtitle carries the argument.
4. **Hero image** — a generic, evocative shot of the product category rather than a specific model. Stock-photo sources (PickPik, Wikimedia) are fine here; the specific-model shots come later. `images/NNN-01.jpeg`.
5. **Byline** as an italic line: `*Written by [Full Name]*`. Present when the piece is guest-authored. See "Guest-author protocol" below.
6. **Purpose paragraph** — ALL CAPS BOLD lead phrase, states what the article does and for whom, and lands a **market-scale statistic with a hyperlink** in the last sentence: "In the United States, [2 million binoculars](url) are sold every year." The statistic sizes the stakes and establishes that the narrator reads the trade numbers.
7. **Promise paragraph** — one or two sentences. Rejects one-size-fits-all, offers the narrator's experience as the instrument, names the payoff.
8. **Doctrine section** — the fork in the category. The single technical choice that organizes everything downstream (porro vs. roof prism; belt vs. direct drive; carbon vs. stainless). Explain both sides, then commit. This section is where the essay earns its authority.
9. **"What to Consider?"** — H2 with ALL CAPS BOLD opener. The buyer's principles: the tradeoff law of the category, the size/brightness/weight axes, the quality-grade vocabulary. Teach the reader to read a spec sheet.
10. **Recommendation sections** — a run of H3s, one per use case, each naming a make, a model, a spec, a price, and a link. See "Recommendation section shape" below.
11. **`## Honorable Mentions`** — bolded-lead micro-entries for adjacent use cases that do not earn a full section: `**Astronomy.** …` `**Marine.** …` `**Vintage.** …`
12. **`## Dishonorable Mentions`** — the category's traps. What not to buy and why. Warranty and returns advice lives near here.
13. **Secrets / synthesis section** — restates the thesis, names the law of diminishing returns for the category, warns about spec inflation from manufacturers.
14. **Numbered tips list** — `### Tips on Better Viewing and Usage`, each item a bolded label and two to four sentences of practical instruction. Six to ten items.
15. **Bold-italic exhortation sign-off** — `***Good Luck and keep on viewing!***`. Warm, imperative, no irony. This is the review spoke's closing move; it replaces the software spoke's wry sigh and the toy spoke's book promo block.

There is **no horizontal rule** in the review spoke, **no Social Post block**, **no book promo block**, and **no dedication line**. The piece is a standalone service article.

Image rhythm: 8–10 images, roughly one per recommendation section. Every specific product recommendation gets its own photograph. Captions are sentence-form, carry a spec or a warning, and end with a parenthetical source: `(Source: [Nikon](url))`, `(Source: [Amazon](url))`, `(Source: [Ebay](url))`. Manufacturer product pages, retailer listings, and auction listings are all legitimate caption sources here in a way they are not in the toy or history spokes.

## Recommendation section shape

Each H3 recommendation section follows the same beats. Two to four paragraphs, no more.

1. **Use case as the header.** "Compact Pocket Binoculars", "Full Size 10X50", "Sports View 7x35", "Zoom Binoculars". The header names the job, not the product.
2. **The verdict sentence, first.** "After careful consideration, the best compacts are roof prism close-focus models." State the conclusion before the reasoning.
3. **Named product with make, model, spec, price, and link.** "For about $100, get a pair of [Nikon Trailblazers](url)." Model naming convention is brand + line + magnification × objective: *Nikon Action EX 7x35*, *Pentax 8.5x21 U-Series Papilio II*, *Coleman 6–16x40*. Always carry the numbers.
4. **The concrete number that proves the claim.** A field of view, a weight, a focus distance. "a wide field of vision for a compact, up to 429 feet", "just over 2 lbs", "can view 1½ feet clearly."
5. **The tradeoff, named plainly.** "The tradeoff is weight." "Their disadvantages include no low-light capability and a really short-to-moderate range." "They are not waterproof nor fogproof." A recommendation without a stated cost is an advertisement.
6. **A second choice at the same price point** where one exists. Gives the reader a fallback when the first pick is out of stock.

## Ownership disclosure — the signature move

The review spoke's primary-source move is that the narrator **owns the thing and says so**. This is the equivalent of the toy spoke's patent scan and the history spoke's site archaeology.

- **Declare ownership explicitly and price it.** "I own a [Flyant 12x25](url) that cost under $100." "I do own a Sogries, a 6x-16x variable 40 porro prism with twist-out eye cups, which is under $100." "I spent under $100 for this unit."
- **Declare non-ownership too.** When the recommendation rests on reviews rather than hands, say so: "Expert reviews have been extremely positive." The reader can weight the two differently.
- **Declare field interviews.** Small-scale reporting earns outsized credibility in a hobby piece: "I interviewed a lifeguard and was told they use the [Bushnell](url) 15x35 offshore." One or two of these per essay.
- **Declare the buying channel.** Facebook Marketplace, eBay, Amazon, the manufacturer's refurbished page. Where the narrator shops is part of the advice.
- **Declare the practice.** "I only go local so I can test them out." "I don't attempt to fix them; instead, I rebuy." Habits reveal the depth of the hobby faster than any credential.

Never recommend a product the narrator has neither owned, handled, nor sourced a review for. If the author has not used it, the draft must ask for that gap to be filled rather than filling it.

## Budget constraint as the spine

The title's price promise is a structural obligation, enforced in every section.

- **Name the ceiling once, then honor it everywhere.** 111 sets "without spending a lot of money" in the title and lands "under $100" or "about $100" in six separate sections. The repetition is the argument.
- **Price every single recommendation.** No naked model names. `$150`, `under $100`, `$200 to $350`.
- **Name the law of diminishing returns for the category, late.** "The law of diminishing returns comes into play when spending hundreds more on roofs for maybe a 10 percent improvement." This belongs in the synthesis section, after the reader has seen the evidence, not in the opener where it reads as a slogan.
- **Let the ceiling justify a skipped feature.** "If your units are under $150, why are warranties necessary?" The budget frame turns a missing feature into a non-issue rather than a compromise.

## Spec literacy — teach one calculation

Every review-spoke essay hands the reader one piece of arithmetic they can carry into a store.

- 111's is the angular-field conversion: "At 1,000 yards, every 1° of angular field corresponds to approximately 52 feet, so it is easy to calculate the field of view."
- State the physical law that the calculation obeys, plainly and without apology: "The higher the magnification, the smaller the field of vision. That is the law of physics, and it cannot be changed."
- Use an everyday analogy once for the hardest concept: "Think of a lens like a window."
- **Glossary in parentheses on first mention.** `BaK4 (barium crown)`, `BK-7 (borosilicate crown)`, `collimation (lens misalignment)`, `Vignetting. Also known as a partial blackout view`. This is the universal 2026-05-30 in-line-descriptor rule doing double duty as the piece's glossary. Every jargon term gets three to six words of translation the first time it appears.
- Give the reader the quality-grade shorthand of the category, then a rule for when it does not apply. 111 insists on BaK4 and then spends a whole section on why vintage BK-7 beats it for stadium use.

## Sources

Review essays cite commercially, not archivally. Inline hyperlinks only, no bibliography, no footnotes.

- **Manufacturer product pages** for the flagship recommendation and its spec sheet.
- **Retailer listings** (Amazon `/dp/` links) for price and availability.
- **Auction and secondary-market listings** (eBay item URLs) for vintage recommendations. These rot; note in the caption what the listing showed so the fact survives the dead link.
- **A trade or press statistic** in the opener for market scale.
- **Reference sources** (Wikipedia diagrams) for the doctrine section's optical or mechanical explanation.
- **Named field interviews** the author conducted.
- **Manufacturer sites for up-and-coming generics** — naming the actual factory brand behind a category is a service to the reader and a hallmark of the spoke.

Reference density: 15–25 hyperlinks per medium-length review, most of them wrapping a product name rather than a verb. Note the contrast with the toy spoke, where the hyperlink wraps the **verb of attribution**; in the review spoke the hyperlink wraps the **product name**, because the reader is meant to click and buy.

## Guest-author protocol

Essay 111 is guest-authored — bylined "Written by Douglas J. Arcuri" and saved with a `.my.md` suffix rather than plain `.md`, the only such file in the corpus. Treat the suffix as the other-byline marker until the author says otherwise.

- **Byline line is mandatory** on a guest piece: `*Written by [Full Name]*`, italic, directly under the hero image.
- **The house structure applies; the house voice does not.** Impose the anatomy, the image rhythm, the caption grammar, the pricing discipline. Do not flatten the guest's diction into the corpus voice.
- **Run the banned-word pass, then negotiate.** Essay 111 as drafted carries a heavy load of banned items — "of course" (three times), "very" (six), "really", "some", "many", "great", "around", "probably", "likely", "can", "allow", "attempts". A guest draft has almost certainly not been through [writing-editing.md](../writing-editing.md). Propose the cuts as a list to the guest author rather than rewriting silently; some of those words are load-bearing to their voice.
- **Fix the mechanical items unilaterally.** Title-case errors, filename/title mismatches, inconsistent unit spacing (`35-50 mm` vs `32–50 mm`), en-dash versus hyphen in ranges, stray zero-width characters. 111 has all four.
- **Ask the guest for the ownership disclosures.** If a recommendation lacks "I own one" or "reviews say", ask before publishing.

## Sentence and paragraph rhythm

- **Paragraph cap: 2–4 sentences.** Tighter than the corpus-wide 3–4-sentence rule, because the reader is scanning for the model name and the price.
- Short declaratives dominate. "The Bushnell is very light." "Compactness has its price." "Their objective lenses are extremely lightweight."
- **Rhetorical question then immediate answer** is the spoke's characteristic turn: "Are there other extra-wide angles on the market today? Of course, the best is 9.3°, not bad for 7x35." Use twice per essay, not more.
- Comparative constructions carry the doctrine section: "In contrast, roof-prism binoculars use a straight-line design." "Once an advantage of roof prisms, waterproofing and fogproofing are now common among modern porro prisms."
- Numbers land at the end of sentences where possible, so the eye catches them while scanning.

## Titles

- **Hobby-entry imperative** is the default: "How to Become a Knowledgeable Binocular Enthusiast Without Spending a lot of Money". The shape is *How to Become a Knowledgeable [Category] Enthusiast Without [Constraint]*.
- **Category survey**: "The Best [Category] Under [Price]" (hypothetical).
- **Contrarian**: "Everything the [Hobby] Forums Get Wrong About [Spec]" (hypothetical).
- **Spec-literacy**: "How to Read a [Category] Spec Sheet" (hypothetical).
- Subtitle is short and functional — "A Practical Guide", "A Buyer's Guide", "What to Own and What to Skip".
- **"A lot" stays banned in body prose, and is an approved exception in a review title.** The 111 title keeps "Without Spending a lot of Money" by the author's decision: the colloquialism *is* the promise, and a corrected phrasing ("Without Overspending", "On a Budget") reads as marketing rather than as a person talking. Reach for the exception only when the banned word carries the reader-facing contract of the piece. Never in the body.

## Diction and signature vocabulary

- **Category jargon without apology, glossed once.** For optics: porro prism, roof prism, objective lens, eyepiece, field of view, FOV, magnification, eye relief, diopter, interpupillary distance, collimation, chromatic aberration, vignetting, exit pupil, close focus, waterproof, fogproof, BaK4, BK-7.
- **Hobby-insider clipping.** "bins" for binoculars. Every hobby has one; find it and use it three or four times so the reader hears the club voice.
- **Recommendation verbs:** recommend, insist on, prefer, grab it, get a pair, go for more, skip, avoid, beware.
- **Judgment adjectives, earned by a number in the same paragraph:** sharp as a tack, incredible, superior, excellent, blurry, a waste of time.
- **Warning phrasing:** "Beware of binoculars sold with high magnifications", "you will know it is a lemon right out of the box", "Do not judge binocular clarity by looking through windows."

## Length and shape

- Short: 800–1200 words for a single-product review.
- Medium: 1500–2500 (111 sits here at roughly 1,900).
- Long: 2500–4000 for a category survey covering six or more use cases.
- Section headers are functional and short — "Compact Pocket Binoculars", "Zoom Binoculars", "On Warranties", "Further Bin Secrets". Two to five words. Use case + spec is the most common shape.
- H2 marks a movement (Consider / Honorable / Dishonorable). H3 marks a recommendation. The ALL CAPS BOLD opener attaches to H2 sections only, not to every H3.

## Porting the spoke to a new product category

The user will ask for this shape applied to other gear. Before drafting a review in a new category, answer these seven questions with the author. Each maps to a fixed slot in the anatomy above.

1. **What is the price ceiling?** One number. It goes in the title and gets honored in every recommendation section.
2. **What is the doctrinal fork?** The one technical choice that splits the category and organizes the essay. Porro vs. roof. Tube vs. solid state. Belt vs. direct drive. Carbon vs. stainless. Mechanical vs. quartz. Prime vs. zoom.
3. **What is the false luxury?** The thing enthusiasts overspend on for a marginal gain. This is the essay's contrarian core and it lives in the synthesis section.
4. **What one calculation should the reader learn?** The arithmetic that turns a spec sheet into a purchase decision.
5. **What are the use cases?** Four to seven. Each becomes an H3. Cover the all-arounder, the portable, the specialist, the budget generic, the vintage/secondary-market pick, and the contrarian pick.
6. **What does the author personally own?** Model, price, and how long. No ownership, no recommendation.
7. **What are the traps?** Two to four. They become Dishonorable Mentions. Include the returns-and-warranty stance.

Then: 8–10 images, one per recommendation, sourced from manufacturer, retailer, or auction listings. Then the numbered tips list. Then the bold-italic exhortation.

Categories this shape is ready for: telescopes, microscopes, film cameras, lenses, turntables and cartridges, headphones and IEMs, mechanical keyboards, chef's knives and sharpening stones, fountain pens and inks, mechanical watches, hand planes and chisels, fishing reels, metal detectors, radio scanners and shortwave receivers, espresso grinders.

## Do

- Own the thing, or say plainly that you have not.
- Price every recommendation, and honor the title's ceiling.
- Name the tradeoff for every product you recommend.
- Gloss every jargon term the first time it appears.
- Take one clear position against the hobby's expert consensus, and defend it with a number.
- Photograph every specific model you name.
- Close with a numbered practical tips list and a warm imperative sign-off.

## Don't

- Don't use "this author" here. That is the toy and history register. Review work is first-person singular and unhedged.
- Don't hedge a recommendation into uselessness. If the piece will not commit, it is not a review.
- Don't recommend a product without a price.
- Don't stack the essay with affiliate-flavored superlatives. Judgment adjectives need a number in the same paragraph.
- Don't apply Social Post blocks, book promo blocks, dedication lines, or horizontal rules. Wrong corpus.
- Don't route a software-tool review here. That is software spoke, structure E.
- Don't silently rewrite a guest author's diction. Propose the banned-word cuts; let them decide.
