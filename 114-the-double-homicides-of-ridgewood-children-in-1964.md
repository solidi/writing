## ON MATERNAL PSYCHOSIS-RELATED CRIMES
# The Double Homicides of Ridgewood Children in 1964
## When Two Mothers Murdered Their Kids Months Apart
#crime, #murder, #america, #newyork, #history

**A RARE DOUBLE-TRAGEDY** occurred in Ridgewood, Queens, New York, in 1964. Two separate women, Doris Hofmeister and Valerie Calia, murdered their children months apart in the once-middle-class American neighborhood.

In May of 1964, Mrs. Hofmeister, mother of four, feared that two of her younger children were mentally incapable - even after the children were evaluated and deemed normal.

Continuing to voice active threats to others, she finally wielded a toilet brush and beat these two of her younger children in their beds, and then suffocated them.

Immediately following, she slit her wrists. In an act of desperation, she then sent her older surviving child to seek help. She said, "Call a neighbor, I've broken my wrists."

![The front page of Mrs. Hofmeister's case, May, 1964. (Source: [Ridgewood Times](https://www.calameo.com/read/006530363f281c27996fd))](images/114-01.jpeg)

In December of the same year and city, another mother, Mrs. Calia, took her husband's unlicensed handgun and shot both children in her home. Then, she took the gun and shot herself in the chest.

She survived by calling the police, stating, "Oh, my God, I've shot myself." The miracle was that her young boy, 10 months old, who took bullets, survived the ordeal.

At the time of the tragedies, both mothers' maternal psychiatric breakdowns were partially hidden from the media. *The New York Times* and AP briefly covered both stories, then stopped reporting on them.

---

## Outcomes of These Maternal Filicide Cases

**BOTH OF THESE** murders remained out of the public eye. What we do know is that Mrs. Hofmeister and Mrs. Calia avoided trial, and instead the mothers were institutionalized for various lengths of time. Records show that Doris Hofmeister was later living in public. She passed away in 2007.

In the case of Valerie Calia, it was later found that her husband was charged with a misdemeanor for the unlicensed possession of the firearm in a New York City home - a violation of the Sullivan Act. Mrs. Calia was institutionalized for an undisclosed time.

Both cases have files under permanent seal and will never see the light of day.

![The front page of Mrs. Calia's case, December, 1964. (Source: [Ridgewood Times](https://www.calameo.com/read/006530363a4ff4d5d1dcb))](images/114-02.jpeg)

And both women avoided a criminal trial, as they were found by court-appointed doctors to be actively psychotic and suicidal. Mrs. Hofmeister and Mrs. Calia were deemed mentally unfit to stand trial and to participate in their own defense.

Following this, both women are believed to have lived full lives after these catastrophes and did not commit further crimes.

## Ridgewood Murders Did Not Set Precedent

**DURING THE 1960s,** mothers in this timeline were actively evaluated for psychosis, and if so, there would be no criminal charges but court-appointed commitment. However, this treatment was never a precedent for default institutionalization; some mothers, although rare, were tried and convicted of first-degree murder.

For instance, [Maggie Young](https://www.findagrave.com/memorial/83858499/maggie_lee-young) (1965) drowned her children. Authorities avoided a criminal trial. After her institutionalization, she escaped and committed suicide afterward.

This author suspects that regional attitudes of compassion influenced Mrs. Hofmeister and Mrs. Calia's outcomes in New York. Back then, media coverage was limited, and the proceedings moved quickly, minimizing the suffering all parties had to endure.

Today, cases like Andrea Yates (2001) and Lindsay Clancy (2023), who murdered their own children and then hurt themselves (before or after), have turned into horrific media spectacles.

![The front page of Mrs. Clancy's case, January, 2023 (Source: The Boston Globe)](images/114-03.jpeg)

Mrs. Yates drowned five children. And Mrs. Clancy, in one of the most brutal cases, strangled three. Afterward, she jumped out of a window and was paralyzed.

Her youngest, Callan, survived three days before being taken off life support. Before the tragedy, Mrs. Clancy sought medical help for suicidal ideation.

Andrea Yates, who attempted suicide numerous times, was convicted of capital murder in the state of Texas, one of the harshest states in which to be sentenced. However, the conviction was overturned, and she later was found not guilty by reason of insanity.

Today, Lindsay Clancy's first trial ended in a mistrial on September 4, 2026. This came after Mrs. Clancy's husband actively defended her on the stand. His 911-recorded tape, also rumored to support her, was kept out of the public record.

Mrs. Clancy is being tried in Massachusetts, a place with compassion. If a plea deal is reached or a second trial is permitted, this author expects that either outcome will end in *not guilty by reason of insanity*.

## Trial-and-Error Cases Will Persist

**THE HOFMEISTER AND CALIA** cases are the only incidents in American history to occur in the same city, in the same year, with similar outcomes.

The Ridgewood cases happened at a time when institutionalization was preferred, but new attitudes against such measures, *deinstitutionalization*, began to take shape.

Unfortunately, this shift in attitude set the stage for extended **trial-and-error** prosecutions. Now, under the universal banner of media voyeurism, these tragedies have become grossly polarized.
