### What Follows Are Unpublished Secrets in the Binocular Optics Industry

​I must tell you right up front that over 95% of binocular units are made in Mainland China and Southeast Asia. These companies promote themselves as generics, and their lens quality is excellent due to improved reproduction processes.

### The Porro Prism Advantage

The Chinese have perfected the porro prism at low cost, and I recommend porro-prism binoculars over roof-prism binoculars. Porro-prisms feature an offset, a "broad-shouldered" shape, in which the objective lenses are spaced wider than the eyepieces, providing a clear sense of depth and superior optical performance.

In contrast, roof-prism binoculars use a straight-line design, making them compact and weather-resistant. Once an advantage of roof prisms, waterproofing and fogproofing are now common among modern porro prisms.

![The internals of a binocular with focus on the porro prism design. (Source: [Wikipedia](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Binocular-optics.png))](images/111-02.jpeg)

Given the roof prism's need for natural brightness, inexpensive units fall short in low light. Porro prisms are better at producing clarity and offer a low-cost FOV that can't be beat.

At lower prices, the porro prisms are easier to manufacture at a higher quality. Expensive lens coatings are not necessary for porro prisms. These coatings don't always address chromatic aberration, which causes unnatural color fringes along the edges.

​Porro prisms correct this aberration, and in my opinion, provide a more realistic depth of field and a stereoscopic 3D effect. A roof prism provides a flat field, covering the subject's requirements. However, we will see that roof prisms have their place in my review, right in a watcher's pocket.

## What to Consider?

**WHEN SELECTING BINOCULARS**, a few sound principles apply. The higher the magnification, the smaller the field of vision. That is the law of physics, and it cannot be changed. When you zoom in, the field of view shrinks. At 1,000 yards, every 1° of angular field corresponds to approximately 52 feet, so it is easy to calculate the field of view.

### Brightness, Glass, and Weight Construction

On brightness, the larger the objective lens, the brighter the view. Think of a lens like a window. A 21–25 mm lens collects less light than a larger 35-50 mm objective lens. There are several sizes in between. For low light, dawn, and dusk, larger objective lenses are a must. 32–50 mm is a good range.

I insist on selecting BaK4 (barium crown), a premium optical glass prism. BaK4 is very sharp and brighter than BK-7 (borosilicate crown). For older vintage wide-FOV binoculars, BK-7 is fine; they were made differently back then. With these facts in mind, the goal is to select durable, high-quality binoculars.

Finally, weight is a major consideration when you are carrying binoculars around, so keep comfort in mind. Around a pound is a good average; however, as the power increases, so does the weight.

### ​Reverse Porro Binoculars

I would call these binoculars microscopes. These units are small and wide, with the objective lens close together and the eyepieces further apart. They are the best near-focus for both porros and roofs. The close-up clarity is superior to that of traditional porros and roof-prism binoculars.

I own a [Flyant 12x25](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0CFKXKY4M) that cost under $100.​ Additionally, the [Pentax 8.5x21](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00SKO4AUY) U-Series Papilio II can view 1½ feet clearly, and is a big seller, definitely one to own.

Their use includes art galleries, butterflies, flowers, and anything you wish to look at up close with magnification. They usually come in 8x to 10x, with lenses ranging from 21 mm to 25 mm. Their objective lenses are extremely lightweight. Their disadvantages include no low-light capability and a really short-to-moderate range. There are many well-known brands out there.

### Zoom Binoculars

In this area of expertise, I find myself disagreeing with the so-called experts. Many are against zoom binoculars because of a significant decrease in field of view caused by the unit's construction, but they do have a point at 10x to 30x.

I do own a Sogries, a 6x-16x variable 40 porro prism with twist-out eye cups, which is under $100. Sogries is a fantastic unit in which the 6x setting has a 305-ft FOV at 1,000 yards. You can start with that and then zero in on your target. It is a sweet spot having a low zoom magnification.

As a second choice, [Coleman](https://www.colemanoptics.com/optics/ca840-l6yc7-hbeyk) has a 6–16x40 for under $100. In addition, the Colemans have fold-up/down rubber eyecups, and their clarity and contrast are excellent.

Regardless of what experts say, low-magnification zoom bins have their place in the viewer's collection. If I know bald eagles have a nest and where they are, zooms are the way to go.

## Honorable Mentions

**Astronomy.** Much higher magnifications and larger-sized objective lenses are recommended. [Celestron](https://www.celestron.com/) is the leader in porros for better light and FOV.

**​Marine.** There are incredible porros for marine use that are waterproof and fogproof. Most come with a 7x50 wide-view, and many have built-in compasses. These porros tend to be expensive, ranging from $200 to $350. I interviewed a lifeguard and was told they use the [Bushnell](https://www.bushnelloutdoors.com/pages/marine) 15x35 offshore.

**Vintage.** The old stuff has been very successful on Facebook Marketplace for quick buys. I only go local so I can test them out. Look for a workable wide-FOV model and make sure it's operable, with no scratches on the lenses or exterior. If you do encounter operational problems, you can unfreeze the main focus and the interpupillary distance control using silicone gel.

![The vintage Bushnell 4x30 PowerView Insta-Focus is sought after. (Source: [Ebay](https://www.ebay.com/itm/257164426060))](images/111-08.jpeg)

The diopter is another story. They are difficult to maintain due to dried-out interior grease. I don't attempt to fix them; instead, I rebuy, as many units are available under $100. Of course, rare German vintage models can fetch in the hundreds based on condition. They are real collector's items.

## Dishonorable Mentions

In my opinion, Auto Permafocus, or "fixed-focus," binoculars have a blurry view up to 30 yards. I do not recommend them as their usage is limited. The concept of not focusing sounds good, but it's a waste of time, and you pay the price of a lack of short-distance clarity.

### ​On Warranties

If your units are under $150, why are warranties necessary? You will know it is a lemon right out of the box, or, if it breaks within 30 days, you can send it back to Amazon for an exchange - no need to be stuck with a lemon from collimation (lens misalignment).

### Further Bin Secrets

This article attempts to cut through the unnecessary minutiae and focus on which binoculars are efficient for each application so you can get the most enjoyment. Beware of binoculars sold with high magnifications; you will know right away if the field of view is correct or if the manufacturer estimated the eye pupil.

Very expensive binoculars are not what they are cracked up to be. The law of diminishing returns comes into play when spending hundreds more on roofs for maybe a 10 percent improvement. They are heavy and miss the 3D stereo effects of the porro prism, no matter how much you spend on roofs. Compactness has its price.

### ​Tips on Better Viewing and Usage

1. **Vignetting.** Also known as a partial blackout view, it can be corrected by purchasing an [Alpine Bino Bandit](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0BHDDTT5L). A genius invention mounted on the eyepieces that acts as a hood, blocking side light. It's like being in a movie theater with the lights off, as it reduces eye fatigue and can be used day or night.

In addition, it prevents the viewer from looking directly at the eyepieces, which can cause smudging and vignetting. Eyepieces with limited depth work well.

2. **Flip-Up Objective Lens Covers.** Loose objective-lens covers can be lost. Flip-up covers remain attached to the barrels and stay out of the way while setting the diopter and main focus.

3. **On Eye Relief.** If you wear glasses, choose a model with at least 15 mm of distance between your eyes and the lenses, and go for more.

4. **Keep Unblocked.** Do not judge binocular clarity by looking through windows, car windshields, or screens.

5. **Focusing Technique.** When using the main focus, use both the middle and index fingers from both hands. This lets you change focus quickly. Use it like a rocker, as its intuitive design guides the direction of focus. It is easier with two hands as you are still supporting the binoculars.

6. **Cleaning Lenses.** I use Zeiss cleaning wipes and never worry about scratching lenses. They can be obtained from [Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0030E4UIQ). In fact, most products discussed in this article are available there.

7. **Special Attachments.** Some manufacturers offer a [camera attachment](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=binocular+smartphone+adapter) that lets you take photos and videos with your smartphone, which is exceptionally good for bird watchers and sightseeing.
